How a piezo printhead forms a droplet

A piezo head does not boil ink or spray it — it deforms a chamber and sends a pressure wave through the nozzle. Drop size, grey-scale levels and dot gain all come from that waveform, which is why two machines with the same head can print differently.

The short answer: a piezo printhead fires by deforming a small ink chamber. A voltage pulse bends a piezo element, the chamber volume changes, and the pressure wave pushes a droplet out of the nozzle. Change the pulse shape and you change the droplet. That single fact explains most of the differences between 'the same head' on two different machines.

One droplet, in four steps

  1. Rest: the meniscus sits at the nozzle with a slight negative pressure holding it back.
  2. Pulse: the piezo element deforms, the chamber contracts, and pressure rises behind the ink.
  3. Ejection: a droplet breaks off; a long tail follows and must break cleanly, or it leaves satellites.
  4. Refill: the chamber refills through the restrictor and the meniscus settles before the next pulse.

The refill step is where speed and reliability are won or lost. Fire too fast and the chamber has not refilled; fire with the wrong waveform and the tail breaks in the wrong place. This is also why ink temperature matters: warmer ink is thinner, refills faster and forms a different droplet at the same voltage.

Grey-scale: one nozzle, several droplet sizes

Modern heads fire more than one drop size from the same nozzle by using multi-pulse waveforms. The Epson i3200-U1 and i1600-U1 in our flatbeds are specified with 3.8 / 6.2 / 9.3 pl at 21.6 kHz in four-level mode, and 5 pl at 43.2 kHz in two-level mode. Smaller drops build smooth gradients and fine detail; larger drops fill solids quickly. The RIP decides which level to use, which is why print quality is never only about the head.

From droplet to dot on the substrate

A 5 pl droplet does not stay 5 pl on the surface. It spreads, and how far it spreads depends on surface energy, ink viscosity, head height and whether the ink was pinned by a pre-cure lamp. That spread is dot gain. If dot gain changes — a new media batch, a colder room, a different white ink — the same file prints lighter or darker, and the operator blames the profile.

Field note

Workshop note: when a print looks grainy in the highlights but fine in the solids, we look at the small-drop levels first. A partially clogged nozzle that still fires the big drop will pass a nozzle check and still ruin a gradient. Printing a 5 % and a 20 % patch tells you more than the nozzle check alone.

The takeaway for buyers is that printhead model is only the start of the specification. Nozzle count and native resolution describe the hardware; waveform, ink and RIP decide what actually lands on the substrate. When comparing two machines with the same head, compare the samples, not the datasheet line.
