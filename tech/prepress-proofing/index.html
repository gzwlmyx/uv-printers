Why every UV job starts with a proof on your substrate

A UV printer needs no plate, which makes it fast — and makes it easy to print a thousand wrong pieces. A physical proof on the customer's own material is the cheapest insurance in the shop.

  • proofing
  • prepress
  • colour approval
  • substrate sample

The short answer: a UV proof is not a check of the file, it is a check of the whole process. The same PDF can look different on two batches of the same board, with a different white build, or under a different lamp setting. Printing one piece on the customer's actual material before the run is the only way to know what the thousandth piece will look like.

What the proof actually settles

ItemWhat can go wrong without a proof
ColourPantone or brand colour drifts between substrates and ink lots
White opacityDark substrate shows through; the design loses contrast
Varnish / reliefGloss level and height are not what the client imagined
AdhesionInk passes on the bench and peels after handling
Size and positionArtwork is right on screen, wrong on the part
Edges and textureThe substrate's grain or coating shows through the ink

How we run a proof

  1. Ask for a real piece of the production substrate, not a similar-looking sample.
  2. Print the actual artwork at the actual resolution and pass count, with the same white and varnish build.
  3. Cure it exactly as the production run will be cured, then let it cool.
  4. Check colour under daylight, then adhesion with tape after 24 hours.
  5. Photograph the proof next to the reference and keep both the photo and the physical sample.
  6. Get written approval before the run; the approved proof becomes the contract sample.
Field note

Workshop note: the proof is also where we discover the material problem the customer did not mention — a warped board, a coating that fish-eyes, a leather that bleeds. Finding it on one piece costs an hour; finding it on 500 pieces costs the order. We keep the approved proof in the job bag until delivery, and if a reprint is needed later, it is the only honest reference.

For overseas buyers, this is the practical reason to send a substrate sample before ordering: the proof is the only artifact that shows what your material does under UV ink, and it travels better than a promise.

One practical rule for export orders: if the customer cannot send material, print the proof on the closest stock you have, label it clearly as a substitute, and list the checks it cannot prove. A labelled substitute is honest; an unlabelled one becomes next month's argument. Keep the signed proof, the substrate sample and a photo of the two together.
