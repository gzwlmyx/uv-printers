Why dark and clear materials need white ink

CMYK inks are translucent. On black acrylic or clear PET the colour has nothing to reflect back, so it turns muddy or disappears. White ink builds the reflective base — and because it is a pigment suspension, it needs circulation, not just a good profile.

  • white ink
  • opacity
  • dark substrates
  • ink circulation

The short answer: colour ink needs a light background to work. CMYK inks are translucent by design — that is how four inks can make thousands of colours — so on a black, dark blue or transparent substrate the light passes through the colour, hits a dark base and never comes back. White ink puts an opaque, reflective layer underneath and the colour suddenly looks like the proof.

Two ways to use white

  • Under-print (white first, then colour, then varnish): the normal route for opaque parts such as black acrylic, dark metal, dark leather.
  • Reverse print (colour first, white last, viewed through the substrate): used on clear film and glass, where the image is printed mirror-image and read from the other side.
  • Spot white: white only behind part of the design, for highlights or to save ink and time.
  • No white: on white or light substrates where the base already does the job.

White ink is a suspension, not a solution

White pigment is dense. Leave a white cartridge or damper still for a few days and the pigment settles; the top turns thin and the bottom turns like cream. That is why machines with white channels run circulation, and why a machine that has been idle over a weekend needs a white purge before a paying job. A weak, streaky white is almost always a circulation or settling problem before it is a head problem.

Field note

Workshop note: we keep the white circulation running through the working day, and we never judge white opacity from a single pass. Two passes of white at a sensible ink limit beat one heavy pass that cure cannot reach through. If the white looks blue-grey, check the lamp and the white ink lot before touching the profile.

For buyers, the practical question is not 'does it print white' but 'how much white, in how many passes, and with what maintenance'. Ask for a sample on your own dark material and look at it under daylight, not under the showroom lamp.

A rough check we use: hold the printed part at arm length under a window. If the colour still reads as the intended hue, the white is doing its job; if it turns grey or olive, the white layer is too thin or not fully cured. When white is printed in two passes, pin-cure between them so the second pass does not push the first one around.
