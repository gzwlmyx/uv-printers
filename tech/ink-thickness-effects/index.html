Ink thickness, dot gain and the eight finishes one machine can make

Ink thickness is a decision with three inputs: the substrate, the artwork and the finish. Change any one and the same file prints differently. Here is how thickness maps to effects, and where it goes wrong.

  • ink thickness
  • dot gain
  • effects
  • relief
  • braille

The short answer: there is no single correct ink thickness. Thickness is set by the substrate (how much it absorbs and how dark it is), the artwork (how saturated and how detailed) and the finish you promised (flat colour, gloss, relief, texture). The same machine can produce all of them; the operator's job is to change one variable at a time.

Three inputs, one setting

  • Substrate: acrylic and coated boards need less ink than uncoated wood or fabric; a dark base needs more white, not more colour.
  • Artwork: deep, saturated images tolerate more ink; fine text and gradients need less, or they fill in.
  • Finish: flat colour is the baseline; gloss needs varnish; relief needs repeated passes on a spot channel.

Eight finishes, honestly described

Dot gain is what makes thickness tricky. A droplet that lands on a smooth, high-surface-energy substrate spreads more than one that lands on a porous board. If you increase ink to make a colour denser, you also increase dot gain, fill the shadows and lose detail. The better move is usually to fix the white base or the profile, not to push more ink through the head.

Field note

Workshop note: acrylic is the material we under-ink the most. On clear or coloured acrylic, a lighter ink load cures faster, looks cleaner at the edges and saves money — as long as the white underneath is right. On uncoated wood the same setting looks washed out, so we keep separate media settings rather than one 'universal' profile.

If a customer asks for 'thicker ink', translate the request: do they want more opacity (white), more gloss (varnish), more height (passes), or a different texture (dot pattern)? Those are four different settings, and only one of them is actually about putting down more colour.
