UV DTF crystal labels: print the sticker, not the part

UV DTF prints the image, white and adhesive onto a film, laminates it, and transfers the whole stack by hand. It is the practical answer for curved, small or already-finished parts that cannot go on a flatbed.

  • UV DTF
  • crystal label
  • transfer
  • curved surfaces

The short answer: UV DTF separates printing from the object. You print a mirrored-or-normal image with white and adhesive onto a PET film, laminate a transfer tape over it, then press the stack onto the part and peel the carrier. The label is a thin, flexible film — so it wraps a bottle or a curved shell that a flatbed cannot reach.

The workflow

  1. Print colour, white and adhesive on the DTF film; the white is what makes the label opaque.
  2. Cure the print fully, then laminate the transfer tape without trapping air.
  3. Clean the part; for low-surface-energy plastics, wipe with IPA and let it dry.
  4. Position the label, press from the centre out, and peel the carrier at a low angle.
  5. Let the adhesive reach full bond — usually a day — before washing or packing.

Where crystal labels fail

Almost all failures are edge lift or trapped air. On a curved part the film has to stretch; if the radius is too tight for the film, the edge lifts within a day or two. On low-surface-energy plastics such as PP or untreated PE, the adhesive has nothing to grab, and a label that looks perfect at packing time is peeling by the time the customer opens the box. A quick IPA wipe and a small trial on the actual part avoids most of this.

Field note

Workshop note: we print a run of test labels first and apply them to the real part — not to a flat sample — before quoting a curved job. The same label that sits flat on an acrylic sheet can lift on a 25 mm-radius bottle shoulder. Also, keep the film and the transfer tape from the same lot: mixing lots changes the peel force enough to matter.

The commercial argument for UV DTF is not only shape. It lets a shop decorate finished goods, keep the flatbed free for rigid boards, and produce short runs of labels without a die. What it does not do is replace a laminated or screen-printed label in a dishwasher or a chemical environment — test before promising.

One colour note: the label is viewed through or against its own white layer, so the same file can look slightly different from a direct flatbed print. Approve the colour on the final part, not on the film. After lamination, let the label sit flat for a day before cutting — cutting while the adhesive is still relaxing is a common cause of lifted corners.
